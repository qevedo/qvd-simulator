# Engineer for Bandwidth, Then Route Around 2ⁿ

On a 24-core i9-14900K with 62 GB of RAM, the fastest possible exact simulator of arbitrary circuits tops out at **31 qubits in double precision or 32 in single precision**. At that scale every gate is a full read-and-write sweep of a 16–32 GiB array through dual-channel DDR5, so speed comes almost entirely from making fewer sweeps. The winning recipe is already known from qsim, Qiskit Aer, Qulacs and the Häner–Steiger 45-qubit work. It combines a single-precision, SIMD-blocked state vector, AVX2 kernels for dense fused 4–5-qubit gates, a measured cost model that drives gate fusion, and cache-blocked "stages" that apply dozens of gates per trip to DRAM. Done well, this should bring a 30-qubit, depth-25 random circuit down to roughly 5–10 s on this machine. That figure is an estimate derived from bandwidth arithmetic and must be measured. Going past 32 qubits exactly means paying with multi-terabyte SSD arrays, lossy compression or approximate fidelity. Exact, generic 40-qubit simulation needs 8–16 TiB of state, and every credible demonstration of it has used clusters or dedicated storage hardware. "40+ qubits on one machine" is real only for structured circuits. Stabilizer, near-Clifford, Pauli-propagation, MPS, tensor-network and decision-diagram methods reach 40 to 20,000 qubits on the circuit classes they suit. The Rust architecture should therefore be built in two halves: first a bandwidth-optimal dense engine, which is also the kernel library for the near-Clifford backend, then a Stim-class stabilizer backend, MPS, and an automatic selector driven by cheap structural indicators.

## Every gate is a memory sweep, and the clock is DRAM bandwidth

A dense state vector needs 2^(n+4) bytes in complex128 ([BMQSim](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14088v1)) and 8 × 2^n bytes in complex64 ([qsim: choose hardware](https://quantumai.google/qsim/choose_hw)). On 62 GB, that means 31 qubits in f64 (32 GiB) or 32 qubits in f32 (32 GiB). A 32-qubit f64 state is 64 GiB, larger than RAM, and Spinoza's authors note that it "would lead to page faults" on a 64 GB box ([Spinoza, arXiv:2303.01493](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.01493)). The kernels must work **in place**, because a second buffer costs a full qubit. JUQCS's 2-byte polar encoding is the only known trick that shrinks memory predictably for any circuit, and on this machine it would allow 34 qubits ([JUQCS-50, arXiv:2511.03359](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03359v3)).

| n | complex128 | complex64 | 2-byte encoded | Fits in 62 GB? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | 16 GiB | 8 GiB | 2 GiB | all |
| 31 | 32 GiB | 16 GiB | 4 GiB | all |
| 32 | 64 GiB | 32 GiB | 8 GiB | f32 and encoded only |
| 33–34 | 128–256 GiB | 64–128 GiB | 16–32 GiB | encoded only |
| 40 | 16 TiB | 8 TiB | 2 TiB | none |

Gate application is memory-bound by a wide margin. A one-qubit gate performs **14 flops per complex amplitude against 32 bytes moved** in double precision, an operational intensity below 0.5 flop/byte ([Häner & Steiger, arXiv:1704.01127](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)). qsim's authors generalize this: a fused q-qubit gate in single precision has intensity 2^(q−1) flop/byte, so "it is beneficial to fuse small gates into larger gates" ([Isakov et al., arXiv:2111.02396](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)). Real implementations sit on this bound. qHiPSTER's 29-qubit double-precision gate ran "very close to" its 0.43 s memory bound at 40 GB/s ([qHiPSTER, arXiv:1601.07195](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.07195)).

For this machine, the arithmetic is as follows. The bandwidth figure is an unverified assumption: dual-channel DDR5 probably sustains about 70–90 GB/s on STREAM, and the first benchmark built should be a Rust STREAM triad that measures it. At that bandwidth, one sweep over a 30-qubit f32 state (17.2 GB of traffic) takes about **0.22–0.25 s**. A 31-qubit f64 or 32-qubit f32 sweep (about 69 GB of traffic) takes about **0.85 s**. Peak compute is around 2.3 TFLOP/s f32 across P- and E-cores, which puts the ridge point near 25–30 flop/byte. Even 5-qubit fused gates (8 flop/byte in f32) are memory-bound in principle. In practice, register pressure (only 16 YMM registers) and shuffle overhead bite earlier, which is why measured optimum fusion widths cluster at 4–5.

The consequence is the organizing principle for the whole design: **runtime ≈ (number of full-state DRAM sweeps) × (sweep time)**. Cache boundaries matter too. Qulacs saw timing jumps "at 17 and 22 qubits" as the state left cache ([Qulacs, arXiv:2011.13524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13524)), and qsim's CPU beats a T4 GPU while a 20-qubit state fits in cache ([arXiv:2111.02396](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)). The 14900K's 36 MB L3 holds roughly a 21-qubit f64 or 22-qubit f32 state, so below that size the engine is compute- and overhead-bound rather than DRAM-bound.

Precision is the cheapest lever. qsim's SIMD backends are single-precision only, and a 2024 cross-package study found **qsimcirq fastest in single precision at large N**. The same study found that Qiskit's and PennyLane's single-precision flags silently kept running in double ([Jamadagni et al., arXiv:2401.09076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09076)). Spinoza measured a 30% gain from f32 ([arXiv:2303.01493](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.01493)). f32 buys the 32nd qubit and halves sweep time. It also carries only about 7 digits of precision, and no source quantifies how f32 error accumulates with circuit depth. The engine should therefore be generic over f32/f64, default to f32 for large states, and accumulate all reductions (norms, sampling cumulative sums, expectation values) in f64, as qsim's sampler does ([qsim statespace_avx.h](https://github.com/quantumlib/qsim/blob/main/lib/statespace_avx.h)).

## Six kernel techniques separate the fastest engines from the rest

### Split real/imaginary blocks remove shuffles from the inner loop

qsim is the only major CPU simulator that stores the state as **eight real floats followed by eight imaginary floats per AVX block**. Amplitude i lives at float offset 16·(i>>3)+(i&7), with the imaginary part 8 floats later ([qsim statespace_avx.h](https://github.com/quantumlib/qsim/blob/main/lib/statespace_avx.h)). Qulacs, QuEST v4, Lightning and Aer all store interleaved `std::complex`. Aer's AVX2 path de-interleaves on load with `permute4x64`/`blend` and re-interleaves on store, so its arithmetic runs on split registers anyway ([qiskit-aer qv_avx2.cpp](https://github.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/blob/main/src/simulators/statevector/qv_avx2.cpp)).

With split registers, a complex multiply-accumulate is exactly four full-width FMAs with no shuffles. Intel P-cores have a single shuffle port, so this matters once fused gates become compute-heavy. The only head-to-head evidence is a 2025 QuEST study that reported about 1.2× from layout alone and about 5× from AVX2 intrinsics. That paper labels its layout inconsistently, as both AoS and SoA ([arXiv:2506.09198](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09198)), so treat the 1.2× as weak evidence. The recommendation is a qsim-style **blocked SoA**: a `#[repr(C, align(64))]` block of 8 f32 real and 8 f32 imaginary values (or 4+4 f64), one cache line each, in a 64-byte-aligned (preferably 2 MiB-aligned) allocation. The cost is an index remap for I/O and no zero-copy NumPy export. Both are rare, end-of-run operations.

### Bit insertion with "high" and "low" target handling

Every simulator iterates over the 2^(n−k) base indices with zeros inserted at the target bit positions. qsim and QuEST v4 do the insertion with one BMI2 `_pdep_u64` ([QuEST bitwise.hpp](https://github.com/QuEST-Kit/QuEST/blob/main/quest/src/core/bitwise.hpp)). qsim's key idea is to split targets into "high" (bit position ≥ 3, outside an 8-lane f32 register) and "low" (bits 0–2, inside the register). For high targets it issues plain vector loads at 2^H offsets. For low targets it makes 2^L−1 lane-permuted copies of each register with `vpermps` and multiplies by a pre-expanded, lane-varying matrix. This avoids per-FMA shuffles entirely. It dispatches monomorphized `ApplyGateH<k>` / `ApplyGateL<H,L>` kernels for gates of up to 6 qubits ([qsim simulator_avx.h](https://github.com/quantumlib/qsim/blob/main/lib/simulator_avx.h)). Aer's AVX2 kernels use the same permuted-copy trick ([qv_avx2.cpp](https://github.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/blob/main/src/simulators/statevector/qv_avx2.cpp)). Lightning instead writes "internal"/"external" variants per gate, which needs up to 2^n functions for an n-qubit gate ([Lightning, arXiv:2403.02512](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02512)). That approach does not scale, and the qsim scheme should be preferred.

Häner and Steiger add a complementary trick. They pre-broadcast the matrix as (m_R, m_R) and (−m_I, m_I) pairs, so each complex MAC costs two FMAs, with the matrix prep amortized over 2^(n−k) uses. They also identify a **cache-associativity hazard**: a k-qubit gate on high qubits touches 2^k lines spaced by large powers of two, which collide in the same cache set once 2^k exceeds the associativity. Remapping frequently targeted qubits to low bit positions "allowed for a 2x decrease in time-to-solution" ([arXiv:1704.01127](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)). The 14900K's P-core L1D and L2 are reportedly 12-way and 16-way. That is unverified here, but if true it suggests k ≤ 4 on high qubits is safe.

### Specialized kernels pay off only when they cut bytes moved

Qulacs ships a large catalogue of kernels: named Paulis, CNOT/CZ/SWAP, diagonal, phase, dense 1/2/multi-qubit, controlled variants, Pauli-string kernels and reversible-boolean permutations. Its paper shows that diagonal, Pauli-rotation and permutation gate times "are independent of the number of target qubits," because every simple kernel is memory-bound ([Qulacs, arXiv:2011.13524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13524)). qsim takes the opposite approach. It has only dense k-qubit kernels plus controlled variants, and it relies on fusion to make everything a dense 4-qubit-ish matrix ([qsim choose_hw](https://github.com/quantumlib/qsim/blob/main/docs/choose_hw.md)).

Both approaches are right in their regime. The specializations that matter are the ones that **reduce traffic**. Controls should be folded into the base-index mask, since "each control qubit halves the needed iterations" ([QuEST cpu_subroutines.cpp](https://github.com/QuEST-Kit/QuEST/blob/main/quest/src/cpu/cpu_subroutines.cpp)). Consecutive diagonal gates should merge into one pass: Rollright "can apply all diagonal gates in a single pass" and eliminated floating-point multiplies for Google circuits ([Markov et al., arXiv:1807.10749](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.10749)). Permutation gates should be pure loads and stores. One trap: control qubits on low bits waste half of each cache line, and qHiPSTER measured about 2× above the memory bound until the control index reached about 10 ([arXiv:1601.07195](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.07195)).

### Threading: saturate bandwidth, then stop

Every C++ engine uses static, contiguous OpenMP partitioning with a serial cutoff: qsim below 1,024 outer iterations ([qsim parfor.h](https://github.com/quantumlib/qsim/blob/main/lib/parfor.h)), Aer at 14 qubits ([qubitvector.hpp](https://github.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/blob/main/src/simulators/statevector/qubitvector.hpp)), Qulacs around 2^13 amplitudes. Qulacs's efficiency "saturates around thread number t = 10" at 15 qubits. It turned super-linear at 22 qubits, where the state fit in L3, and fell again above that ([arXiv:2011.13524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13524)). On Sapphire Rapids, Lightning's AVX-512 kernels stopped scaling past about 16 threads. Switching to non-temporal stores helped at high thread counts and hurt at low ones ([arXiv:2403.02512](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02512)).

No simulator has been benchmarked on Intel's hybrid P/E cores. Intel's generic guidance is to run on P-cores only for predictable performance ([Intel oneMKL guide](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/docs/onemkl/developer-guide-linux/2023-1/managing-performance-with-heterogeneous-cores.html)). A Linux bug report shows the scheduler sometimes "always prefers E cores" ([kernel bugzilla 218195](https://bugzilla.kernel.org/show_bug.cgi?id=218195)), so placement must be explicit. The reasoned position is this. Eight P-cores probably saturate dual-channel DDR5 for streaming kernels. E-cores help mainly for compute-heavier fused 4–5-qubit gates. Static equal splits make E-cores stragglers, so work-stealing over L2-sized chunks (2^14–2^16 amplitudes, thousands of tasks) is the safer default. The three configurations (8 P-cores, 16 P-threads, all 32 threads) must be benchmarked per kernel width.

### Sampling and expectation values take one or two extra sweeps

For terminal measurements, qsim draws S uniforms, sorts them, and sweeps the cumulative |amp|² once, accumulating in double. That makes shots almost free: "Multiple samples can be generated with minimal additional cost" ([qsim statespace_avx.h](https://github.com/quantumlib/qsim/blob/main/lib/statespace_avx.h); [qsim overview](https://github.com/quantumlib/qsim/blob/main/docs/overview.md)). Its sweep is single-threaded. Aer instead builds a 2^10-block prefix index and searches per shot in parallel ([qubitvector.hpp](https://github.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/blob/main/src/simulators/statevector/qubitvector.hpp)). The better design combines both: one parallel pass computes per-chunk sums, then each thread merge-walks the sorted uniforms falling in its own chunk. That gives two sweeps for any number of shots.

Pauli-string expectation values need no state copy. Aer and Qulacs compute an X flip mask, a Z parity mask with popcount, and a global i^(#Y) phase, and visit each pair (i, i⊕x_mask) once ([Qulacs stat_ops_expectation_value.cpp](https://github.com/qulacs/qulacs/blob/main/src/csim/stat_ops_expectation_value.cpp)). Grouping Hamiltonian terms that share an x_mask into one pass is an obvious extension that neither implements. It cuts passes from the number of terms to the number of distinct X patterns.

### Published numbers set the bar, but no clean same-CPU table exists

qsim's documented CPU benchmark runs depth-20 random circuits with fusion f=3 at maximum threads on a 60-vCPU cloud VM. It reports **30 qubits in 13.5 s and 34 qubits in 292 s** ([qsim: choose hardware](https://quantumai.google/qsim/choose_hw)). Rollright ran a 30-qubit, 524-gate Google circuit in **71 s on a 4-core MacBook** in f32 ([arXiv:1807.10749](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.10749)). Häner–Steiger's single-node 30-qubit depth-25 run took 9.58 s, 14.8× faster than qHiPSTER ([arXiv:1704.01127](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)). Single-gate benchmarks show Yao, ProjectQ and Qulacs converging above 20 qubits, suggesting "they all reached the top performance" for gate-by-gate simulation ([Yao.jl, arXiv:1912.10877](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10877)). That is the point of the whole argument: once one kernel saturates bandwidth, only fusion and blocking move the needle. None of these numbers come from the same CPU, so a "fastest" claim requires running qsim, Aer and Qulacs locally.

## Fusion and cache blocking cut DRAM sweeps by 5–30×

### Fusion is a dynamic program over a measured cost table

All serious engines fuse runs of small gates into dense k-qubit unitaries. qsim uses greedy growth capped at f=2–6, with **f=4 "typically optimal" for large circuits and many threads** and f=6 "very rarely optimal" ([arXiv:2111.02396](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396); [qsim fuser_mqubit.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/quantumlib/qsim/master/lib/fuser_mqubit.h)). Qiskit Aer runs a pipeline of diagonal fusion, then 1- and 2-qubit absorption, then a **dynamic program** `costs[i] = min_j cost(j..i) + costs[j−1]`. Its AVX2 per-width cost table is 1, 1, 1.1, 3 and 5.83 for k = 1–5, with a 5-qubit cap ([Aer fusion.hpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/main/src/transpile/fusion.hpp)). That table implies a 5-qubit fused gate pays off only if it absorbs about six or more sweeps' worth of gates.

Häner–Steiger's depth-25 30-qubit circuit (369 gates) collapses to about **82, 46 or 36 clusters at k_max = 3, 4 or 5**, roughly 10 gates per 5-qubit cluster ([arXiv:1704.01127](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)). Atlas (SC'24) generalizes the DP over two kernel types: a fused dense matrix with a benchmarked cost per width, or a "shared-memory" kernel that loads a block once and applies gates one by one at cost α + Σcost(g). It beat greedy 5-qubit fusion on most circuit families and was **up to 20.2× (4.0× average) faster than HyQuas** ([Atlas, arXiv:2408.09055](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)). Qulacs, by contrast, found 2-qubit merging optimal on its older CPUs ([arXiv:2011.13524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13524)). Every serious system **benchmarks its cost table on the target hardware**, so the Rust engine should do the same at install time.

### Cache-blocked stages turn dozens of gates into one DRAM pass

The bigger win at large n comes from blocking. The approach partitions the qubits into b "local" qubits, whose 2^b-amplitude blocks fit in cache, and n−b global ones. It groups the circuit into stages in which every non-diagonal gate touches only local qubits, and runs each stage block by block, in parallel, with all of the stage's gates applied while the block is cache-resident. Between stages, a qubit permutation brings the next working set local. Doi and Horii's implementation in Aer gained 20% on Quantum Volume and 80% on QFT at 33–35 qubits ([arXiv:2102.02957](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.02957)).

Häner–Steiger needed only **two global-to-local swaps for a 42-qubit depth-25 circuit**, and diagonal and CZ gates on global qubits needed no swap at all ([arXiv:1704.01127](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)). Atlas formalizes this as "insular" qubits (controls, and the qubits of diagonal gates) that may stay non-local, and finds a provably minimal number of stages with an ILP that solves in seconds ([arXiv:2408.09055](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)). On a single CPU, Fatima and Markov's diagonal batching and cache-line clustering were 20× and 12× faster than Microsoft QDK and Qiskit ([arXiv:2008.00216](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.00216)). A 2026 cluster study combining blocking, a "merge booster" and fusion reported 34× over naive at 36 qubits ([Wang et al., arXiv:2604.12256](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.12256)).

No paper reports CPU-only L2/L3 staging on a modern desktop, so the translation to this machine is my own design reasoning. Use b ≈ 15–16 (an L2-resident block of 256 KiB–1 MiB) as the inner tier, and b ≈ 20 as an L3 tier. Keep the three lowest qubits always local so that every gather reads whole cache lines, as Atlas and HyQuas do. Keep a logical→physical qubit map instead of physically permuting: SWAP gates become relabels (Qiskit's `ElidePermutations` does the same, per [Qiskit passes](https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/docs/api/qiskit/transpiler_passes)). **Fold each permutation into the next stage's gather** rather than paying a separate sweep. With this design the cost becomes (stages + unfolded permutations) sweeps rather than (fused gates) sweeps.

### Cheap pre-passes shrink n and gate count before any of this

Qiskit's transpiler provides reference implementations for barrier and identity removal, inverse and commutative cancellation, 1-qubit chain merging, diagonal removal before measurement, `LightCone` pruning to the gates that affect a measurement or observable, and block consolidation ([Qiskit transpiler passes](https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/docs/api/qiskit/transpiler_passes)). Light-cone pruning is especially valuable because every qubit it drops halves memory and time.

### Noise and mid-circuit measurement need deferral to keep fusion alive

qsim's trajectory simulator defers Kraus operators whenever a precomputed lower bound allows, which preserves gate fusion. It reports "an order of magnitude runtime speedup for low noise with 27 qubits." High noise "prevents gate fusion and limits the arithmetic intensity" ([arXiv:2111.02396](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)). Measurements are fusion barriers in qsim and Aer. For many shots with mid-circuit measurement, Aer offers a shot-branching mode that shares common prefixes ([AerSimulator docs](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-aer/stubs/qiskit_aer.AerSimulator.html)). At 30+ qubits on 62 GB, branches must be processed depth-first.

## Past 32 qubits, exactness costs storage, fidelity or generality

### SSD out-of-core works, but not on one consumer NVMe

Storage-backed simulation produces exact results and has real records. SnuQS ran a 42-qubit supremacy circuit on "a workstation-level system with many NVMe SSDs and HDDs," against an in-memory workstation limit of about 34 qubits. It relies on circuit partitioning, I/O prefetching and a three-step permutation for large contiguous transfers ([SNU Thunder lab](https://thunder.snu.ac.kr/?p=875)). Keio and Tsukuba reached **up to 43 qubits with an FPGA board and 32 SATA disks** ([Keio press release](https://www.keio.ac.jp/en/press-release/20231211-1/)). BMQSim reached 47 qubits by spilling compressed blocks to SSD, on structured circuits ([BMQSim](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14088v1)). Plain OS swap underperforms badly ([Edinburgh MSc project](https://project-archive.inf.ed.ac.uk/msc/20247458/msc_proj.pdf)).

On this machine the arithmetic is unforgiving. The disk has about 49 GB free, which cannot hold even a 33-qubit f32 state (64 GiB). One read-plus-write pass over a 40-qubit f32 state (8.8 TB) at 7/5 GB/s would take about **50 minutes**, versus about 3 minutes from DRAM, before SLC-cache exhaustion slows the drive. Each such pass writes about 8.8 TB, so a consumer drive's endurance rating would be consumed within about a hundred passes. Out-of-core mode is a later, optional backend that requires a dedicated multi-TB NVMe. It should reuse the stage scheduler, since global-qubit permutations are exactly what force disk passes.

### Compression buys 2–4 qubits on hard circuits and 10+ only on easy ones

Wu et al.'s SC19 compressed simulator shows the pattern clearly ([arXiv:1911.04034](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.04034)). Its 61-qubit result is **Grover-specific**, at a compression ratio of 7.39×10⁴. Random circuits reached only 45 qubits at depth 11, at a ratio of 6.03. On a single 192 GB node, a 36-qubit random circuit took 7.96 h at fidelity 0.933, and a 36-qubit QFT took 78.98 h. Compression and decompression consumed 63–96% of the runtime. The authors state plainly that "it does not work as well on random circuits." BMQSim reached 42 qubits on one 128 GB workstation for GHZ, BV and cat states, but only **36 for QFT and 35 for QAOA and Ising** ([arXiv:2410.14088](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14088v1)).

JUQCS's 2-byte adaptive encoding is the predictable middle path. It saves a fixed 8× versus complex128, which means 34 qubits here, at about 2–3× compute cost. It can still show visible errors: the 50-qubit adder gave expectation values of 0.48 or 0.52 instead of 0.5 ([arXiv:2511.03359](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03359v3)). On a CPU whose kernels are already bandwidth-bound, a lightweight fixed encoding is more attractive than SZ-style compressors, which make the run compressor-bound.

### Hybrid Schrödinger–Feynman trades memory for paths

Cutting the circuit into two halves costs about 2^d·(2^|A| + 2^|B|) for d cut gates ([simulator survey, arXiv:2311.16505](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.16505)). Memory stays near 2^(n/2), so 50–60 qubits fit easily, but runtime explodes with the number of cut gates. Rollright estimated about **$35k of cloud compute for 1M amplitudes of a 56-qubit depth-40 circuit at 0.5% fidelity**, with cost scaling linearly in fidelity ([arXiv:1807.10749](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.10749)). MQT DDSIM's DD-based variant brought some hard circuits from more than a day to minutes ([Burgholzer et al., arXiv:2105.07045](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2105.07045)). It yields amplitudes or low-fidelity samples, never the full state.

## Structure, not hardware, unlocks 40 to 20,000 qubits

| Method | Wins on | Demonstrated reach | Cost driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stabilizer tableau + Pauli frames (Stim) | Clifford circuits with Pauli noise, QEC | ~20,000 qubits, 8M gates in ~15 s, then ~1 kHz full-shot sampling ([Stim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.02202)) | O(n) per gate, O(n²) per random measurement |
| Stabilizer rank / ZX decomposition | Clifford+T with few T | 50–100 qubits with ≤70 T; hidden shift with 1,000+ T in minutes ([arXiv:2109.01076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01076); [arXiv:2202.09202](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09202)) | ~2^(0.396·t) terms |
| Clifford frame + active state vector (Clifft) | Low-magic QEC, magic-state cultivation | Exact end-to-end cultivation over hundreds of billions of shots on CPU ([arXiv:2604.27058](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27058)) | 2^(peak active qubits) |
| Pauli propagation | Local observables; noisy, shallow or small-angle circuits | IBM 127-qubit "utility" experiment on one laptop core ([arXiv:2306.16372](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16372)) | Number of Pauli strings after truncation |
| MPS | 1D/quasi-1D, low entanglement, approximate RCS | 54-qubit 2D CZ circuits at ~1%/gate error on a laptop ([arXiv:2002.07730](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07730)); 64 qubits, depth 10 in ~10 s ([quimb](https://quimb.readthedocs.io/en/latest/circuit/circuit-mps.html)) | O(χ³) per 2-qubit gate |
| Tensor-network contraction | Amplitudes, batches, light-cone expectation values of shallow circuits | Sycamore 53q/20 cycles needed 512 GPUs for ~15 h ([arXiv:2111.03011](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.03011)) | Contraction width and flops |
| Decision diagrams | Oracle/arithmetic-heavy algorithms | 100-qubit GHZ, 64-qubit basis-state QFT, 40-qubit Grover in 1,240 s, 37-qubit Shor in 5,586 s, ≤260 MB ([Zulehner & Wille](https://www.cda.cit.tum.de/files/eda/2018_tcad_advanced_simulation_quantum_computation.pdf)) | Unpredictable; ~2^n on random circuits |

The stabilizer backend is the highest-value addition after the dense engine. Stim tracks the inverse tableau, so deterministic measurements cost O(n) rather than O(n²). It spends 11 bitwise ops per 256 Paulis multiplied, and it targets about **0.01 CPU cycles per qubit per Clifford gate**. Its batched Pauli-frame sampler pushes hundreds of shots through each AVX2 word. Stim beats CHP, Qiskit's stabilizer method and Cirq by one or more orders of magnitude, and by many orders when sampling ([Gidney, arXiv:2103.02202](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.02202)). AVX2 is exactly the width Stim was designed around. A graph-state simulator wins only on circuits made of many small, independent pieces ([Anders & Briegel](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0504117)).

Near-Clifford methods bridge the gap. Their cost is exponential only in the non-Clifford count t: about 2^(0.5t) for exact probabilities and 2^(0.23t) for approximate sampling ([Bravyi & Gosset, arXiv:1601.07601](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.07601)), and about 2^(0.396t) with ZX cat decompositions, which ran 50-qubit, T-count-1,400 hidden-shift circuits "in a couple of minutes on a consumer laptop" ([arXiv:2202.09202](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09202)). quizx is already a Rust port of PyZX and runs a chain-fusion benchmark in 17 ms versus 98 s in Python ([quizx](https://github.com/Quantomatic/quizx)). The newest design, Clifft, is the one that fits this project best. It factors the state into a Clifford frame, a Pauli frame and a **dynamically sized active state vector** that grows at non-Clifford gates and shrinks at measurements. It stays within a constant factor of standard tools at both the pure-Clifford and fully non-Clifford extremes ([arXiv:2604.27058](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27058)). In other words, it is Stim plus the dense kernels, and both are things this project builds anyway. tsim's own benchmark shows the scale of the stakes: on an 85-qubit, 10-T distillation circuit, Stim (with S in place of T) took 0.14 µs/shot, while tsim took 14.59 µs/shot on CPU ([QuEra tsim blog](https://www.quera.com/blog-posts/introducing-tsim-fast-non-clifford-circuit-simulation-for-qec-researchers)).

For expectation values, Pauli propagation is cheap and powerful. Cliffords never branch, and each anticommuting Pauli rotation branches at most once. Coefficient and weight truncation keep it tractable. A 6×6 Ising model over 30 Trotter layers took 35 s to 26 min on one thread ([Rudolph et al., arXiv:2505.21606](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21606)). Its weaknesses are many-controlled gates, real-time dynamics, and states with very broad Pauli support. MPS remains the workhorse beyond 32 qubits for near-1D circuits. Lazy qubit permutation (quimb's `CircuitPermMPS`) cut a 64-qubit run from 25 s to 10 s and also reduced the error ([quimb docs](https://quimb.readthedocs.io/en/latest/circuit/circuit-mps.html)). On 62 GB, χ ≈ 2,000–4,000 is the practical ceiling for about 100 qubits (my arithmetic). Tensor-network contraction with hyper-optimized paths gave >10⁴× better Sycamore estimates ([Gray & Kourtis, arXiv:2002.01935](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.01935)). On a workstation it suits only shallow or light-cone-limited networks with largest intermediate around 2^32 elements. Decision diagrams have genuine wins on Grover and Shor, but their size cannot be predicted cheaply, and many of their famous wins (GHZ, QFT on a basis state) are product or stabilizer states that other backends handle trivially.

The existing state of the art in automatic selection is thin. Qiskit Aer chooses density matrix for noisy, small, many-shot circuits, stabilizer if the circuit is all-Clifford, and otherwise the first memory-feasible method from statevector, density matrix and MPS. It never picks extended stabilizer automatically, and it never prefers MPS while the state vector fits ([aer_controller.hpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/main/src/controllers/aer_controller.hpp)). PRISM-Q similarly routes Clifford circuits to its stabilizer backend and oversized circuits to MPS with χ = 256 ([prism-q](https://docs.rs/crate/prism-q/0.27.0)). Every method above has a cheap pre-computable indicator: Clifford-ness, T-count (after ZX reduction), peak active qubits, light-cone size, cut-crossing gate counts that bound χ, and contraction width. That leaves clear room for a better selector.

## Rust delivers C++-class kernels if unsafe stays inside them

Stable Rust is now adequate for this work. Since Rust 1.86, safe functions can carry `#[target_feature]` ([Rust 1.86](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2025/04/03/Rust-1.86.0/)). Since 1.87, most `std::arch` intrinsics are callable from safe code inside such functions, with only pointer loads and stores remaining `unsafe` ([Rust 1.87](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2025/05/15/Rust-1.87.0/)). AVX-512 intrinsics stabilized in 1.89, so a future server port needs no nightly ([Rust 1.89](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2025/08/07/Rust-1.89.0/)). `std::simd` remains nightly-only ([std::simd](https://doc.rust-lang.org/std/simd)). For runtime dispatch, `multiversion` ([docs](https://docs.rs/multiversion/latest/multiversion/)) or `pulp`, which faer uses ([pulp](https://docs.rs/pulp/latest/pulp/); [faer](https://docs.rs/crate/faer/latest)), cover portability. For a single-machine build, Spinoza's recipe is simplest: `-C target-cpu=native`, `lto`, `codegen-units = 1`, `panic = "abort"` ([arXiv:2303.01493](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.01493)).

Spinoza argues that hand-written SIMD is unnecessary because the work is memory-bound. That holds for one-qubit sweeps, but not for the compute-heavier fused 4–5-qubit kernels that this design depends on, which is why PRISM-Q ships explicit AVX2/FMA/BMI2 kernels ([PRISM-Q performance guide](https://docs.rs/crate/prism-q/0.28.0/source/docs/guides/performance.md)). Use `cargo-show-asm --mca` to verify the emitted `vfmadd` sequences ([cargo-show-asm](https://github.com/pacak/cargo-show-asm)).

The supporting crates are mature. `num_complex::Complex<T>` is `#[repr(C)]` and layout-compatible with `[T; 2]`, which helps when exporting data ([num-complex](https://docs.rs/num-complex/latest/num_complex/struct.Complex.html)). Rayon's `with_min_len` controls task granularity ([rayon](https://docs.rs/rayon/latest/rayon/iter/trait.IndexedParallelIterator.html)). PRISM-Q engages Rayon at 14 qubits with at least 4,096 elements per task, and reports that hyperthreading helps above 24 qubits by hiding latency. Linux exposes P-cores and E-cores in `/sys/devices/cpu_core/cpus` and `/sys/devices/cpu_atom/cpus` ([kernel perf docs](https://gbmc.googlesource.com/linux/+/refs/heads/linux-6.4.y/tools/perf/Documentation/intel-hybrid.txt)), and `core_affinity` can pin threads ([core_affinity](https://docs.rs/crate/core_affinity/^0.8)). For memory, allocate the state via anonymous mmap with `MADV_HUGEPAGE` and pre-fault it in parallel; `memmap2` exposes `HugePage` and `PopulateWrite` advice ([memmap2 Advice](https://docs.rs/memmap2/latest/memmap2/enum.Advice.html); [kernel THP docs](https://docs.kernel.org/admin-guide/mm/transhuge.html)). Lazily zeroed pages would otherwise make the first gate pay millions of page faults.

No Rust simulator has published a credible multithreaded benchmark against qsim, QuEST or Aer. Spinoza compared single-threaded against Qulacs 0.6.0 and other packages ([arXiv:2303.01493](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.01493)). LogosQ compared only against Python, Julia and Q# libraries ([arXiv:2512.23183](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23183v2)). PRISM-Q is the most engineered: seven backends, fusion, tiling and auto-dispatch, but no cross-simulator numbers on the pages read. roqoqo-quest is simply QuEST behind FFI ([roqoqo-quest](https://docs.rs/roqoqo-quest/)), which makes it a convenient same-machine C baseline. Correctness needs as much attention as speed. One Rust crate's Rz had the opposite sign convention, which gave fidelity 0.0 against its peers on a QAOA test ([dev.to benchmark](https://dev.to/cleiton_augusto_/we-benchmarked-4-rust-quantum-simulators-three-agreed-one-didnt-4dd2)). PRISM-Q's bit order is reversed relative to Qiskit. For front ends, Qiskit's `openqasm3_parser` crates parse about 80× faster than the reference parser ([openqasm3_parser](https://github.com/Qiskit/openqasm3_parser)), and PyO3/maturin is the standard Python bridge.

## Build the dense f32 engine first, the Stim-class backend second

The prioritized plan below follows directly from the evidence. Most runtime at 28–32 qubits is DRAM sweeps. Most qubits beyond 32 come from structure, and the dense kernels are reused by the near-Clifford backend. Fixed thresholds in this plan are engineering starting points, to be replaced by measured values on this machine.

| Priority | Component | Key design decisions | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| P0 | Benchmark and correctness harness | Rust STREAM triad (≥4 GiB, pinned, THP); scalar reference simulator; cross-checks against Aer/qsim with explicit bit-order and gate-convention tests; criterion/divan micro-benches | Measured bandwidth B known; reference agrees with Aer to 1e-12 (f64) |
| P1 | Dense SV core | Generic over f32/f64 with f32 default; 64-B blocked SoA (8 re + 8 im f32); in-place; mmap + MADV_HUGEPAGE + parallel first touch; qsim-style H/L-split AVX2 dense kernels for k = 1–5 via pdep; diagonal, permutation and mask-folded controlled kernels; f64 reductions | 1-qubit sweep reaches ≥85% of B at 30 qubits |
| P1 | Threading | Rayon pool pinned via sysfs P/E topology; L2-sized chunks with work-stealing; serial below ~14 qubits; benchmark 8P / 16T / 32T per kernel width | Best config chosen per k from data |
| P1 | Measurement layer | Two-pass parallel sorted-uniform sampling; collapse; Pauli expectation grouped by x_mask; logical→physical qubit map | Shots add ≤2 sweeps regardless of count |
| P2 | Circuit optimizer | Barrier/identity removal, SWAP relabeling, light-cone and idle-qubit pruning, inverse/commutative cancellation, 1-qubit merging, diagonal batching (incl. CX–diag–CX ladders) | Gate count and n reduced before scheduling |
| P2 | Fusion DP | Aer-style DP over a per-width cost table **measured at install time**; Atlas-style choice between dense-fused and cache-resident "apply-sequentially" kernels; Häner bit-location remapping for hot qubits | Sweeps for a 30q depth-25 RQC ≤ ~46 (Häner k=4 cluster count) |
| P2 | Cache-blocked stages | L2 tier b ≈ 15–16 and L3 tier b ≈ 20; insular (diagonal/control) qubits stay global; permutations folded into stage gathers; greedy staging first, ILP later | DRAM sweeps ≈ stages, not fused gates |
| P3 | Stabilizer backend | Stim architecture: bit-packed AVX2 tableau, inverse tableau, batched Pauli-frame sampler, Pauli noise | Within ~2× of Stim on surface-code sampling |
| P3 | Auto-selector | Task type → Clifford check → T-count / peak active qubits → light-cone size → SV memory check → MPS χ bound → TN width; report fidelity for approximate backends | Picks the measured-fastest backend on a benchmark suite |
| P4 | MPS backend | χ-capped TEBD with lazy permutation, faer SVD, truncation-error fidelity tracking | 64-qubit depth-10 random circuit within quimb's ~10 s |
| P4 | Near-Clifford (Clifft-style) | Clifford frame + Pauli frame + active SV reusing P1 kernels; optional ZX T-count reduction via quizx | Degrades gracefully to Stim (t=0) and dense SV |
| P4 | Pauli propagation | Bit-packed (u128 / [u64;k]) strings in a hash map; coefficient and weight truncation; `expectation(observable)` entry point only | Reproduces published 127-qubit kicked-Ising values |
| P5 | Optional extensions | Noise trajectories with qsim-style deferral; 2-byte encoded SV (34 qubits); TN contraction for amplitudes; out-of-core SSD via the stage scheduler (needs dedicated multi-TB NVMe); DD backend with a node cap | Only if workloads demand them |

The selector should treat backends as estimates competing on predicted cost, not as a fixed rule chain like Aer's. All-Clifford circuits with Pauli noise go to the tableau regardless of n. Expectation-value tasks first compute the observable's reverse light cone. If it fits in about 30 qubits, the selector simulates only the light cone densely; if not, it uses Pauli propagation for noisy or near-Clifford circuits and tensor contraction otherwise. Circuits with few non-Clifford gates, or a small peak active dimension, go to the near-Clifford backend. Anything up to 31–32 qubits otherwise runs on dense SV, which is the robust default. Above that, the selector computes an MPS bond bound from gate counts crossing each cut under a bandwidth-minimizing qubit order, and a quick tensor-network path search, and refuses or warns rather than silently swapping. All thresholds should be calibrated from benchmarks on this machine, since no published study maps these indicators to measured runtimes.

The benchmarking targets follow from the bandwidth model. At 30 qubits in f32, a single sweep should take about 0.25 s, near measured STREAM. Fused k ≤ 3 gates should cost the same as a one-qubit sweep, and k = 4 should cost at most about 2× (Aer's own table charges 3×). A 30-qubit depth-25 random circuit should finish in about 10 s with fusion alone (about 46 sweeps × 0.23 s) and about 5 s with cache blocking. Both figures are projections, not measurements. The 31-qubit f64 and 32-qubit f32 runs must complete without swapping. Below about 14 qubits, per-gate overhead should approach Yao's and Qrack's, the low-overhead leaders ([arXiv:2401.09076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09076)). The decisive comparison is qsim, Qulacs and Aer built natively on the same 14900K with matched precision, threads and circuits (QV, QFT, depth-20/25 random circuits, QAOA, Trotterized Ising). Results should be reported as sweeps executed, achieved GB/s as a fraction of STREAM, and wall time.

## Conclusion

The research reframes "fastest simulator" as an accounting problem rather than a kernel-tuning problem. Once a one-qubit kernel hits about 85–90% of DRAM bandwidth, and Yao, ProjectQ and Qulacs all converge there, further speed comes only from executing fewer sweeps: fusion with a measured cost model, cache-resident stages, folded permutations and single precision. The largest reported gains (12–15× for Häner–Steiger, up to 20× for Atlas, 34× for the 2026 blocking study) come from these schedule-level techniques. SIMD intrinsics, worth about 5× in the QuEST study, only lift a kernel up to a bandwidth bound that it can never pass. Two questions remain open, and this project can be the first to answer them: whether the hybrid P/E design wants E-cores at all, and how well CPU-only L2/L3 staging works on a desktop.

The second reframing concerns qubit count. On this machine, the honest exact ceiling is 32 qubits (34 with lossy-but-bounded encoding). Every larger number is bought with structure. The most leveraged architectural decision is therefore to make the dense engine reusable: as the full simulator up to 32 qubits, as the active-subspace engine inside a Clifft-style near-Clifford backend, and as the light-cone engine for expectation values. Paired with a Stim-class tableau and an MPS backend behind a cost-based selector, that gives a Rust simulator that is fastest where dense simulation is possible and still useful at 40, 100 or 20,000 qubits where the circuit allows it, while reporting exactly which regime each answer came from.
