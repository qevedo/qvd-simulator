# Gate fusion, circuit scheduling, cache blocking and qubit reordering for state-vector simulation

Scope: algorithms and cost models that reduce memory traffic in state-vector (Schrödinger) simulators, with a single 24-core AVX2 CPU and 62 GB RAM in mind. Sources are primary (papers and source code) unless noted. "Inferences" mark my own reasoning, not sourced claims.

Notation: n = number of qubits, k (or q, f) = qubits in a (fused) gate, b = "local"/blocked qubits (chunk size 2^b amplitudes).

---

## 1. Gate fusion: how qsim, Qiskit Aer, Qulacs, Häner–Steiger and Atlas fuse gates, and the published speedups

### Takeaway
Every serious simulator fuses runs of 1- and 2-qubit gates into dense k-qubit unitaries, so the state is swept once per fused gate instead of once per gate. qsim does this greedily with a hard cap of 2 to 6 qubits (4 recommended for large circuits on many threads). Qiskit Aer runs a dynamic-programming pass over a per-width cost table, defaulting to at most 5 fused qubits, plus a separate diagonal fuser. Qulacs uses "light" (subset-merge) and "heavy" (commute-then-merge) greedy passes. Atlas (SC'24) replaces the heuristics with an exact DP over "kernels" that can be either fused matrices or cache-resident gate sequences.

### Cited Findings

**qsim (Google)**
- The fusion cost argument from qsim's authors: a q-qubit gate on an n-qubit state is 2^(n−q) independent (2^q × 2^q)·(2^q-subvector) products; total flops ≈ 2^(q+2)·2^(n+1) against 2^(n+4) bytes read+written in single precision, so arithmetic intensity = 2^(q−1) flop/byte. "The arithmetic intensity is small for small values of q and the performance is usually limited by the memory bandwidth. It is beneficial to fuse small gates into larger gates to increase the arithmetic intensity." — [Isakov et al., "Simulations of Quantum Circuits with Approximate Noise using qsim and Cirq", arXiv:2111.02396, §III.A–B](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)
- qsim's fusion has two steps. (1) Large and small gates that are time-neighbours on the same qubits are combined, e.g. 1-qubit gates into 2-qubit gates. (2) Greedy growth: "the first unmarked gate is picked and marked. The nearest (unmarked) neighbors (the gates that share qubits with the picked gate) forth in time and the next nearest unmarked neighbors back in time (if they do not have unmarked neighbors further back in time) are added while the resulting fused gate is no greater than the specified maximum fuse size f." — [arXiv:2111.02396 §III.B](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)
- "Typically the optimal value of the maximum fuse size f is 4 for large numbers of threads (or on GPUs) and large circuits. Smaller fuse size, f = 2 or f = 3, can be optimal for small numbers of threads and/or small circuits." — [arXiv:2111.02396](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)
- qsim hardware guide: on CPU, keep f at 2–3 for circuits up to 22 qubits; "f=4 is the best option" for 22+ qubits; f=2 or 3 can be optimal with few threads (up to 4–8); "f=6 is very rarely optimal." Its published CPU benchmark uses depth-20 random circuits, f=3, max threads (c2-standard-60: 13.455 s at 30 qubits, 291.987 s at 34 qubits). Multithreaded simulation "benefits from high-bandwidth memory (above 100GB/s)". Fewer than 17 qubits run on one thread because of OpenMP overhead. — [qsim docs, "Choose hardware"](https://quantumai.google/qsim/choose_hw)
- `MultiQubitGateFuser` (lib/fuser_mqubit.h): `max_fused_size` can be 2 to 6 (clamped with `min(6, ·)`). The header comment says "a value of 4 or 5 is recommended to balance memory bandwidth and computational overhead". The phases are: (a) visit gates in decreasing size, each absorbing adjacent smaller gates whose qubits are a subset of its own (`FusePrev`/`FuseNext`); (b) if `max_fused_size > 2`, `FuseGateSequences`/`FindLongestGateSequence` combine chains on different qubit sets, searching forward and backward with an early exit once a candidate reaches `max_fused_size`; (c) `FuseOrphanedGates` greedily pairs leftovers. Measurements and controlled gates are unfusible barriers, and control qubits block fusion across them. `times_to_split_at` plus measurement times define independent fusion windows. There is no explicit cost model, only the size cap and "biggest first". — [qsim source, fuser_mqubit.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/quantumlib/qsim/master/lib/fuser_mqubit.h)
- `BasicGateFuser` (deprecated) fuses only 1- and 2-qubit matrix gates: a 2-qubit gate absorbs neighbouring 1-qubit gates on its qubits, and leftover 1-qubit gates become single-qubit fused gates. "Non-matrix gates (such as user-defined controlled gates) and matrix-gates acting on more than two qubits are not fused." — [qsim source, fuser_basic.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/quantumlib/qsim/master/lib/fuser_basic.h)
- qsim's SIMD kernel stores real and imaginary parts in separate SIMD registers to avoid horizontal ops (k = 8 floats per register for AVX). It splits gate qubits into "high" (index ≥ log2 k) and "low". All-high gates vectorize directly, computing k matrix-vector products at once. Low qubits need in-register permutations p = (i⊕k)|(i&m̃), which give k/2^l products per pass. qsim uses single precision by default. — [arXiv:2111.02396 §III.C](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)

**Qiskit Aer (IBM), src/transpile/fusion.hpp**
- Defaults in code: `fusion_enable=true`, `fusion_max_qubit=5`, `fusion_threshold=14` (fusion runs only when the circuit has more than this many qubits), `fusion_cost_factor=1.8`, `fusion_parallelization_threshold=10000` ops. Diagonal fusion is on, with `_fusion_min_qubit=3`. Cost-based fusion is on. `_fusion_cost_1.._10` override the per-width costs. — [Qiskit Aer fusion.hpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/main/src/transpile/fusion.hpp)
- Fuser pipeline order: `DiagonalFusion` → `NQubitFusion<1>` → `NQubitFusion<2>` → `CostBasedFusion`. `NQubitFusion<N>` takes an N-qubit op and absorbs neighbours backward and forward whose qubits lie inside its set. `DiagonalFusion` recognizes `p, cp, u1, cu1, mcu1, rz, rzz`, `diagonal` and u3(0,0,λ), plus the CX-ladder / diagonal / reversed-CX-ladder pattern (e.g. the CX–RZ–CX form of exp(−iθZZ…)). It emits a single diagonal op of 3 to max qubits. — [fusion.hpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/main/src/transpile/fusion.hpp)
- Cost model `estimate_cost(from..until)`: a diagonal span costs 1.0. Otherwise, with AVX2 the costs are n=1,2 → 1.0, n=3 → 1.1, n=4 → 3, n≥5 → 1.8^(n−2) (n=5 → 5.83). Without AVX2 the cost is 1.8^(n−1). `CostBasedFusion` is a DP: `costs[i] = min over j and width ≤ max_fused_qubits of estimate_cost(j..i) + costs[j−1]`, with ties broken toward fusing, then a traceback. Segments are cut at ops the method cannot fuse (measure, reset, etc.). — [fusion.hpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/main/src/transpile/fusion.hpp)
- The AerSimulator docs list `fusion_max_qubit` and `fusion_threshold` with default `None`, meaning the value is set automatically per simulation method. The defaults above (5 and 14) are the statevector values in code. — [AerSimulator docs](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-aer/stubs/qiskit_aer.AerSimulator.html)
- An IBM Tokyo technical report (Horii and Doi) combines gates by pattern matching, greedy algorithms and a graph algorithm. It notes that gate fusion does not always improve simulator performance. (Summary from search results only; the report itself was not read.) — [IPSJ QS21002023](https://ipsj.ixsq.nii.ac.jp/record/210570/files/IPSJ-QS21002023.pdf)

**Qulacs**
- Cost model: T_gate ≈ T_over + max(N_com/V_FLOPS, N_mem/V_BW). Merging gates on M1 and M2 changes arithmetic from 2^(n+|M1|)+2^(n+|M2|) to 2^(n+|M1∪M2|) and memory ops from 2^(n+1) to 2^n. Computing the fused matrix costs at most O(2^(3|M1∪M2|)). "We should perform merge operations until N_mem/V_BW is balanced to N_com/V_FLOPS. The optimal size of merged quantum gates is relevant to … BF ratio … it is typically optimal to merge quantum gates until every quantum gate acts on at most two qubits in the case of random quantum circuits." — [Suzuki et al., "Qulacs", Quantum 2021, arXiv:2011.13524 §5.1, §5.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13524)
- "Light" optimization greedily merges neighbouring pairs where one gate's targets are a subset of the other's (no arithmetic penalty). "Heavy" optimization also commutes gates into adjacency, using per-qubit commuting Pauli-basis tags (e.g. CNOT commutes with Z on control and X on target), and merges while the merged target count ≤ a given block size. CPTP maps and parametric gates are not merged. — [arXiv:2011.13524 §5.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13524)
- Qulacs threads only the outer B0 loop and disables multithreading below an empirical qubit threshold. — [arXiv:2011.13524 §5.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13524)

**Häner & Steiger (SC'17), "0.5 Petabyte Simulation of a 45-Qubit Quantum Circuit"**
- A 1-qubit gate costs 14 flop per complex output entry against 16 B read + 16 B write, so operational intensity is < 1/2 and the workload is "memory-bandwidth bound on most systems". "As long as the application remains memory bound, larger gates can be applied in (almost) the same amount of time … multiple gates acting on k different qubits can be combined into one large k-qubit gate." — [Häner & Steiger, arXiv:1704.01127 §3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- Kernel tricks: permute matrix entries once so that qubit indices are sorted. Block the 2^k-dim mat-vec over register-sized blocks, with block size B chosen by an autotuning/code-generation loop. Precompute (m_R, m_R) and (−m_I, m_I) so that each complex MAC is 2 FMAs with no in-loop permutes. Use OpenMP `collapse` so few outer iterations still scale. Use NUMA-aware first touch. — [arXiv:1704.01127 §3.1–3.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- Clustering inside a stage: "We greedily try to increase the number of qubits k within a cluster while still maintaining the condition that k ≤ kmax, where kmax is the largest k for which the k-qubit gate kernel still shows good performance on the target system", with a small local search for the largest cluster before assigning leftovers. A 7-gate example fuses into one 3-qubit gate. The pass takes 1–3 s in Python. — [arXiv:1704.01127 §3.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- Table 1 (depth-25 supremacy circuits, 30 local qubits), as parsed from the PDF text. Total gates are 369 / 447 / 528 / 569 for 30 / 36 / 42 / 45 qubits. Clusters needed for 30 qubits are about 82 (kmax=3), 46 (kmax=4) and 36 (kmax=5), about 10 gates per 5-qubit cluster. Larger circuits follow a similar ratio. — [arXiv:1704.01127 Table 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)

**Atlas (Xu, Cao, Miao, Acar, Jia, SC'24)**
- KERNELIZE is a dynamic program over ordered sets of open kernels, with a weak-convexity/monotonicity constraint so that kernels can be executed in sequence. Each kernel is one of two types. A "fusion" kernel multiplies all gates into one matrix, with cost a benchmarked constant per qubit count. A "shared-memory" kernel loads a batch of amplitudes into fast memory and applies the gates one by one, with cost α + Σ_g COST(g), where α is the load cost. A simpler O(|C|²) DP variant is DP[i+1] = min_j DP[j] + COST(C[j..i]). — [Atlas, arXiv:2408.09055 §V, App.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)
- Atlas compared KERNELIZE against a baseline that "greedily packs gates into fusion kernels of up to 5 qubits, the most cost-efficient kernel size in the cost function" on A100. The greedy baseline is fine for dj and qsvm circuits "but it does not generalize to other circuits". Preprocessing averages 7.2 s per circuit (ILP staging 3.3 s, KERNELIZE 3.9 s, single thread). — [arXiv:2408.09055 §VII](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)
- Atlas's shared-memory kernels always include the three least-significant qubits, following HyQuas, so that each load is at least a contiguous run of 8 amplitudes. — [arXiv:2408.09055 footnote 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)

**Published speedups attributable to fusion / kernel optimization**
- Häner & Steiger, vs the qHiPSTER depth-25 runs: 14.8× (30 qubits, 1 node, 9.58 s), 12.8× (36 qubits, 64 nodes, 28.92 s), 12.4× (42 qubits, 4096 nodes, 79.53 s). The 45-qubit run took 552.61 s on 8,192 KNL nodes at 0.428 PFLOPS, with 78% of the time in communication. These speedups combine kernels, fusion and swap scheduling. — [arXiv:1704.01127 Table 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- 2026 HPC-cluster paper (Wang, Wang, Tu, Hung, NTU): for QFT, "Naive_f achieves a 2.4× performance improvement over Naive when gate fusion is enabled". Their full framework (cache blocking + "merge booster" + fusion) reaches 34× over naive at 36 qubits and 5.8× over a hybrid baseline. "When gate fusion is utilized, the Aer Simulator … achieves a speedup of at least threefold in single-machine", and a diagonal-specialized simulator gets more than 9.7× on QAOA. — [Wang et al., arXiv:2604.12256 §5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.12256)
- HyQuas (Tsinghua) chooses a method per circuit part (OShareMem shared-memory grouping vs TransMM matrix-multiply-based), reporting "up to 10.71× speedup on a single GPU and 227× speedup on a GPU cluster" over prior systems. — [HyQuas project page](https://pacman.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/~zjd/projects/hyquas/); [GitHub](https://github.com/thu-pacman/HyQuas)
- Atlas is "up to 20.2× (4.0× on average) faster than HyQuas, 7.2× (3.2× on average) faster than cuQuantum", and up to 105× faster with DRAM offload. — [arXiv:2408.09055 §VII](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)

### Inferences
- For a Rust implementation, a good structure is: (1) a cheap Qulacs-light / qsim-phase-1 subset-absorption pass; (2) an Aer-style DP (`costs[i] = min_j cost(j..i)+costs[j−1]`) over a topologically ordered gate list, with a per-width cost table **measured on the target machine at startup or install** (Atlas and Häner both benchmark rather than guess); (3) a separate diagonal pass. The DP is O(|C|·window·max_qubits) and runs in milliseconds for typical circuits.
- Aer's AVX2 cost table (1, 1, 1.1, 3, 5.83 for k=1..5) implies a 4-qubit fused gate costs as much as 3 one-qubit sweeps and a 5-qubit gate as much as about 6. Fusion to k=5 therefore pays only if it absorbs about 6 or more gates' worth of sweeps. This matches Häner's Table 1 (about 10 gates per 5-qubit cluster on supremacy circuits) and qsim's "4 is usually best".
- Atlas's two kernel types matter for CPUs. A "shared-memory kernel" on a CPU is a cache-resident sweep: load a 2^b-amplitude block into L2, apply many unfused small gates, write back. Its cost is one memory pass plus per-gate cache-bandwidth costs, so wide but sparse gate groups don't pay the 4^k flop growth of a dense fused matrix.

### Gaps
- I did not read the Yao.jl, KQCS, Intel-QS (qHiPSTER), Q-GPU, "Diffuse" or QuEST fusion implementations, so they are not characterized here.
- I found no paper titled "Gate fusion is map coloring for quantum circuit simulation". Searches returned nothing matching, so it may not exist under that title.
- I found no IBM/Ishikawa 2022+ paper dedicated to Aer's fusion cost model. The cost numbers above come from source code only, and the origin of the constants (1.1, 3, 1.8) is undocumented.
- I could not find qsim measurements that sweep f = 2..6 on the same CPU. The docs give only qualitative guidance.

## 2. Choosing the fusion width: arithmetic intensity vs memory bandwidth (roofline)

### Takeaway
Applying a dense 2^k×2^k gate reads and writes every amplitude once (constant traffic), while flops grow as 2^k per amplitude (4^k per 2^k-subvector). Arithmetic intensity is about 2^(k−2) flop/byte in double precision (2^(k−1) in single). Fusing is free until the kernel reaches the machine's ridge point, which in practice falls at k ≈ 4–5 on AVX2/AVX-512 CPUs, because real kernels hit register pressure and permute overheads well before peak flops.

### Cited Findings
- qsim: flops ≈ 2^(q+2)·2^(n+1), bytes = 2^(n+4) (single), so AI = 2^(q−1). — [arXiv:2111.02396 §III.A](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)
- Häner & Steiger: 14 flop per output amplitude for a 1-qubit gate, 32 B traffic, so AI < 1/2. The kmax choice "depends on the peak performance, the memory-bandwidth, the cache-size & associativity of the system, and the circuit to simulate". Roofline plots for Edison (12-core Xeon E5-2695 v2, AVX, 230.4 GFLOPS peak) and KNL (3133.4 GFLOPS peak) show the 1-qubit kernel deep in the memory-bound region and the 4-qubit kernel near the ridge. — [arXiv:1704.01127 §3.1–3.3, Fig. 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- Cache associativity limit: k-qubit kernels on high-order qubits touch 2^k cache lines spaced 2^m apart (m = lowest gate qubit). "a 2^k-way cache should map the corresponding cache-lines to different locations". Performance drops for high-order targets "when 2^k is larger than the set-associativity of the last-level cache" (KNL L2 is 16-way, shared by 2 cores). — [arXiv:1704.01127 §3.3, Fig. 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- HiSVSIM gives the same analysis for CPUs: 28 flops per 1-qubit 2×2 product vs 64 B moved, so AI = 7/16, memory-bound. With "a L3 cache (e.g., 32MB) shared by all cores, a L2 cache (e.g., 1MB) per core", performance above about 21 qubits (2^21·16 B = 32 MB) "would be degraded by the capacity cache misses on L3 cache". — [Fang et al., HiSVSIM, arXiv:2205.06973 §II](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.06973)
- Qulacs: merge "until N_mem/V_BW is balanced to N_com/V_FLOPS" (the "BF ratio"). For random circuits on their CPUs, merging up to 2 qubits was typically optimal. — [arXiv:2011.13524 §5.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13524)
- qsim reports that a whole 20-qubit wavefunction fits in CPU cache (c2-standard-60), where the CPU beats a T4 GPU. At 24+ qubits the CPU becomes RAM-limited. — [arXiv:2111.02396 §III.F](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)
- Aer's AVX2 cost table (k=1..5: 1, 1, 1.1, 3, 5.83) encodes the empirical view that up to 3 qubits are effectively free (memory-bound) and 4+ become compute-bound. — [fusion.hpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/main/src/transpile/fusion.hpp)
- Atlas measured 5 qubits as "the most cost-efficient kernel size" for fusion kernels on A100 (cuQuantum). — [arXiv:2408.09055 §VII-E](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)

### Inferences (worked roofline for the target machine; derived, not sourced)
- Per amplitude, a dense k-qubit gate does 2^k complex MACs = 8·2^k flops (4·2^k FMAs counted as 2 flops each), and moves 32 B (complex128 read+write). AI_double = 2^k/4 = 2^(k−2): k=1 → 0.5, 2 → 1, 3 → 2, 4 → 4, 5 → 8, 6 → 16 flop/B. Single precision (complex64, 16 B) doubles these values.
- Example machine: a 24-core AVX2 desktop CPU with 62 GB usable RAM is plausibly an Intel Core i9 13th/14th gen (8 P-cores + 16 E-cores, AVX2 only, dual-channel DDR5, roughly 70–90 GB/s achievable). This is an assumption to verify with `lscpu` and STREAM. Theoretical DP peak is roughly 0.8–1.2 TFLOP/s (P-cores 2×256-bit FMA, E-cores 1×). Ridge point ≈ 1000/80 ≈ 12 flop/B, so k≈5–6 in theory. Real complex-matvec kernels reach perhaps 30–50% of peak, because of shuffles and because AVX2's 16 YMM registers cannot hold a 2^k subvector for k≥4. The **practical optimum is likely k=4 (maybe 5) for ≥ 26-qubit states**, and k=2–3 when the state fits in L3 (≤ ~21 qubits). Measure it: benchmark k=1..6 kernels on 28–30-qubit states, then put the measured times into the DP cost table.
- Memory capacity: 62 GB holds at most 2^31 complex128 amplitudes (32 GiB), i.e. **31 qubits in double** or 32 qubits in complex64 (32 GiB), leaving room for OS and buffers. An out-of-place algorithm needing a second buffer would cut this by one qubit, so prefer in-place kernels (Häner notes in-place halves memory).
- With about 80 GB/s, one full sweep of a 31-qubit double state (64 GiB of traffic) costs ~0.85 s. A 30-qubit state costs ~0.43 s per sweep. Total runtime ≈ (#sweeps) × that, so every eliminated sweep is the unit of optimization.
- On hybrid P/E-core parts, use static partitioning with care. Work-stealing (rayon) over chunks avoids stalls from the slower E-cores.

### Gaps
- I found no published measurement of fused-kernel throughput vs k specifically on a 24-core hybrid Intel or Zen 4 AVX2 desktop. The numbers above are estimates and must be measured.
- Haner's exact roofline numbers (GB/s) for Edison and KNL were in figures and could not be extracted reliably from the PDF text.

## 3. Cache blocking and qubit reordering: making qubits "local", stages, and the cost of permutation

### Takeaway
All the blocking schemes work the same way. Partition the n qubits into b "local" qubits (amplitudes indexed by them form a contiguous 2^b block that fits in fast memory) and n−b "global/non-local" qubits. Group the circuit into **stages** in which every non-diagonal gate touches only local qubits. Execute each stage block by block (each block independently, so this parallelizes across cores), applying all the stage's gates while the block is cache-resident. Between stages, a qubit permutation (swap of local↔global bit positions) brings the next needed qubits into the local set; it costs about one full-state pass (an all-to-all on clusters). Häner–Steiger and Doi–Horii use greedy heuristics. Atlas solves staging optimally with an ILP and kernelizes each stage with a DP.

### Cited Findings

**Häner & Steiger (cluster, but the same structure as cache levels)**
- Local qubits (index < l) need no communication. Two schemes exist: keep global qubits global and do pairwise exchanges per global gate, or "swap global qubits with local ones, apply gates to local qubits in the usual fashion". A q-qubit global-to-local swap is one (group) all-to-all, and "we expect the global-to-local scheme to perform better". To swap arbitrary local qubits, they first do local swaps that move them to the highest local positions. — [arXiv:1704.01127 §3.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- Scheduling, step 1: minimize the number of global-to-local swaps by finding the largest "stage" of gates executable without communication. "A depth-25 42-qubit supremacy circuit requires only two global-to-local swaps", and the swap count is "mostly independent of the number of local qubits (29, 30, 31, or 32)". By default they swap with the lowest-order local qubits, plus "a cheap search … to find better local qubits to swap with" (36 qubits: 2 swaps → 1). — [arXiv:1704.01127 §3.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- Global gate specialization: CNOT/CZ/T/diagonal gates on global qubits need no communication. A CZ becomes a conditional phase or local Z depending on rank, and a T becomes a rank-dependent phase. With this specialization 42- and 45-qubit circuits need 2 swaps instead of 3. — [arXiv:1704.01127 §3.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- Steps 2–3: cluster gates within a stage into ≤ kmax-qubit fused gates, then move global-to-local swaps earlier when that removes small trailing clusters without adding swaps. — [arXiv:1704.01127 §3.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- Qubit-to-bit-location mapping heuristic (to avoid the associativity penalty on high-order bits): "Assign the qubit to bit-location 0 such that the number of clusters accessing bit-location 0 is maximal. From now on, ignore all clusters which act on this qubit and assign bit-locations 1, 2, and 3 in the same manner…" This "allowed for a 2x decrease in time-to-solution". — [arXiv:1704.01127 §3.6.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- MCDRAM blocking (KNL's 16 GB fast memory acting as a cache) "requires a sequence of several gates acting on qubits below bit-location 29". Performance dropped when few k-qubit gates ran between swaps. Their 4-qubit kernel reached half the MCDRAM bandwidth (≈2× DRAM). — [arXiv:1704.01127 §4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)

**Doi & Horii, "Cache Blocking Technique to Large Scale Quantum Computing Simulation on Supercomputers" (IEEE QCE 2020, arXiv:2102.02957), implemented in Qiskit Aer**
- The state is divided into 2^(n−nc) chunks of 2^nc amplitudes. "If consecutive gates perform qubits that are smaller than the number of chunk bits nc, any data movements do not occur while simulating them." Qubits never touched by gates are mapped to positions ≥ nc ("bit reordering"). — [arXiv:2102.02957 §II.B, §IV.A](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.02957)
- Transpiler algorithm (Listing 3): while gates remain, "choose QB_CHUNK[nc-1] qubits to store in chunk" (chosen from qubits used by upcoming multi-qubit gates), emit noiseless `chunk_swap` gates to bring them in, then scan the remaining gates. A gate whose qubits are all in-chunk and not blocked is emitted. A multi-qubit gate straddling the chunk marks its qubits blocked, and blocked gates go to the next round. Sections are delimited by `begin_blocking`/`end_blocking`. "The algorithm … is kind of an heuristic algorithm and it is not the best optimized solution because we are applying the cache blocking transpilation on the fly." — [arXiv:2102.02957 §IV.A](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.02957)
- Execution (Listing 4): for a blocking section, each chunk is fetched (CPU→GPU if needed), all gates in the section are applied, and the chunk is swapped out. Chunks are independent, so the loop "can parallelize … by mapping to multiple GPUs or multiple threads on a CPU". `chunk_swap(sq0,sq1)`: if sq0 < nc, half of the amplitudes are exchanged between a pair of chunks. If both are ≥ nc, whole chunks are exchanged (four-chunk case). Only one extra buffer chunk per memory space is needed. — [arXiv:2102.02957 §IV.C](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.02957)
- Gate application is memory-bound ("byte/flop ≈ 2.29 in double precision"). Measured gains on IBM AC922 (6× V100, 512 GB host): about 20% faster for Quantum Volume and 80% faster for QFT when chunks spill to CPU memory (33–35 qubits). Strong and weak scaling across up to 16 nodes improved, and CPU memory can act as backing store with the GPU as a "cache". — [arXiv:2102.02957 §V](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.02957)
- Aer options: `blocking_enable` (default False), `blocking_qubits` ("the qubit number for chunk, should be smaller than the smallest memory space"), with the sizing rule "sizeof(complex)*2^(blocking_qubits+4) < size of the smallest memory space", and `chunk_swap_buffer_qubits` (default 15). Example: "30-qubits circuit is distributed into 2^10 chunks with 20-qubits." — [Aer GPU how-to](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-aer/howtos/running_gpu.html); [AerSimulator docs](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-aer/stubs/qiskit_aer.AerSimulator.html)

**Atlas (SC'24): optimal staging + kernelization**
- Qubits are split into L local (inside a GPU), R regional (inside a node) and G global. STAGE solves a binary ILP that splits the circuit into at most s stages and assigns a local/regional/global partition per stage so that each gate's **non-insular** qubits are local. The objective counts local and global qubits that change between stages, with the inter-node cost factor c = 3. The ILP is solved with PuLP+HiGHS, and the stage count is provably minimal (Theorem 1). Between stages Atlas does an all-to-all qubit remapping. — [arXiv:2408.09055 §IV, §VII-A](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)
- "Insular" qubit: for a 1-qubit gate, the qubit is insular if the gate is diagonal or anti-diagonal. For controlled gates, control qubits are insular. Insular qubits can stay non-local (inspired by Häner's global-gate specialization), so diagonal and control-qubit work never forces a swap. — [arXiv:2408.09055 §IV](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)
- Atlas always produced no more stages than SnuQS's heuristic, and unlike SnuQS never got worse when the local-qubit count increased (23 → 24). — [arXiv:2408.09055 §VII-D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)

**HiSVSIM (Fang et al., PNNL/GaTech, 2022)**
- HiSVSIM compiles the circuit to a DAG and partitions it with **acyclic** graph partitioning (dagP) into parts whose qubit sets fit in a faster memory layer. Each part is simulated as a smaller state-vector computation. Reported gains are up to 3.9× (2.1× average) over the baseline with dagP, and multi-level partitioning adds up to 5.7×. — [arXiv:2205.06973](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.06973)

**Fatima & Markov, "Faster Schrödinger-style simulation of quantum circuits" (HPCA 2021, arXiv:2008.00216), single-node CPU**
- The paper exploits data- and thread-level parallelism and CPU cache "by reordering circuit gates". Diagonal gates are batched, "simulating all diagonal gates in one memory pass over amplitudes". Same-type diagonal clusters (CZ, T) are encoded as bitmasks and simulated with bitwise and mod-p instructions plus Gray-code traversal. Non-diagonal 1-qubit gates are clustered so "we read entire L1 cache lines from memory and apply multiple gates to a cache line when possible". — [arXiv:2008.00216 §1, §4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.00216)
- Clustering by reordering: a topological scan from each unclustered gate g_k marks qubits "obstructed" or "unobstructed". Same-kind gates on unobstructed qubits are reordered toward g_k. Commuting different-kind gates are skipped over. Non-commuting gates obstruct their qubits. "Diagonal gates can always be reordered without affecting circuit functionality." — [arXiv:2008.00216 §4.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.00216)
- Results: 20× and 12× faster than Microsoft QDK and Qiskit/QASM on 5×5 arrays, 3.27× less memory than QDK, and "up to 4000× speedups over QDK and earlier versions" on some workloads. — [arXiv:2008.00216 abstract](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.00216)

**Wang et al. 2026 (cache blocking + "merge booster" + fusion)**
- Even a gate block confined to 4 of 8 qubits normally costs 21 × 2^8 updates (each gate sweeps the full state). If the block is "entanglement-free" with respect to the outside qubits, it can be simulated on a 2^4 sub-state and recombined by tensor product (19 × 2^4 updates in their example), at the cost of extra buffers. Fusion size was "restricted … to fit the processor cache". — [arXiv:2604.12256 §3, §5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.12256)

### Inferences (CPU translation; derived, not sourced)
- **Cache-resident sweep on one CPU.** Pick b so that a block of 2^b amplitudes fits in per-core L2 with room to spare. With 1–2 MB L2 and complex128 (16 B), b = 15–16 (512 KB–1 MB). A shared-L3 tier (~32–36 MB) allows b ≈ 20–21. Each stage = an ordered list of (fused) gates whose non-diagonal qubits all lie in the low b bit positions. Execution: `par_chunks_mut(1<<b).for_each(|blk| for g in stage { apply(g, blk) })`. DRAM traffic per stage = one read + one write of the state, regardless of how many gates the stage holds. Diagonal gates and controls on high (non-local) bits can be applied inside the block by computing the block's high-bit index once (Atlas's "insular" idea and Häner's global-gate specialization).
- **Two-level blocking.** Inside an L2-resident block, still fuse into k ≤ 4 dense gates, since the L2→register path is also bandwidth-limited, only about 5–10× faster than DRAM. Alternatively, use Atlas's "shared-memory kernel" mode: apply unfused gates one by one while the block stays in L1/L2 (cost α + Σ cost_g). The DP picks per group.
- **Permutation pass cost.** Swapping a set of high (global) bit positions with low (local) ones is a bit permutation of indices. It can be done out-of-place in one streaming read+write pass (~one 1-qubit gate sweep), or in place via pairwise swaps of sub-blocks (Doi's chunk_swap: when one bit is local and one global, swap half-blocks between chunk pairs). Better still, **fold the permutation into the next stage's block load**. Gather the block's amplitudes from their strided old locations, run the stage, and scatter back to the new layout. Then the permutation costs no extra sweep, only less-sequential access (gathering runs of ≥ 2^3–2^4 contiguous amplitudes keeps cache lines whole; Atlas/HyQuas likewise always keep the 3 lowest qubits local). Keep a logical→physical qubit map (`perm[q]`) instead of physically restoring order until the final readout.
- **Staging heuristic for a Rust MVP.** Use Doi–Horii's greedy (choose the next local set from upcoming multi-qubit gates and look ahead) together with Häner's "choose swap partners that are least needed soon" (a Belady-like rule). An optimal ILP as in Atlas is possible offline, since Atlas's ILP solves in seconds. Weight each stage boundary as 1 sweep (or 0 if folded) against the number of fused-gate sweeps saved.
- **Expected gain.** Runtime drops from (#fused gates) sweeps to (#stages + #extra permutation passes) sweeps. On 30+ qubits, Häner's random circuits needed only about 2–3 stages for 25 cycles with 29–32 local qubits. With b = 16–21 on a CPU there will be more stages, but each holds dozens of gates. This is an inference: the reduction factor is circuit-dependent and must be measured.
- **Associativity hazard.** Avoid kernels whose 2^k touched lines share a cache set. Fused gates on high bits within a block should be few, or remapped to low bit locations with Häner's greedy bit-location assignment.

### Gaps
- I found no paper reporting CPU-only (L2/L3) cache blocking results for a modern x86 desktop. Doi–Horii and Atlas evaluate GPU/cluster hierarchies. Fatima–Markov is CPU but focuses on L1-line clustering and diagonal batching, not stage permutation.
- Intel-QS (qHiPSTER) cache-blocking specifics, Q-GPU's pruning/reordering, and HyQuas's OShareMem details were not read in primary form.
- I found no published measured cost of an in-place bit-permutation pass relative to a 1-qubit gate sweep on CPU. My "≈ one sweep" estimate is analytical.

## 4. Circuit-level optimizations before simulation

### Takeaway
Before scheduling, cheap symbolic passes shrink the circuit: merge 1-qubit chains, cancel inverse pairs (also through commutation), merge or batch diagonal gates, drop diagonal gates right before computational-basis measurement, drop gates outside the light cone of the measured qubits or observable, and remove idle qubits. Qiskit ships each of these as a named transpiler pass that can serve as a reference implementation.

### Cited Findings
- Qiskit transpiler passes: `RemoveDiagonalGatesBeforeMeasure` ("Remove diagonal gates (including diagonal 2Q gates) before a measurement"); `InverseCancellation` ("Cancel specific Gates which are inverses of each other when they occur back-to-back"); `CommutativeCancellation` ("Cancel the redundant (self-adjoint) gates through commutation relations"); `CommutativeInverseCancellation` ("Cancel pairs of inverse gates exploiting commutation relations"); `Optimize1qGatesDecomposition` ("Optimize chains of single-qubit gates by combining them into a single gate"); `Optimize1qGatesSimpleCommutation`; `RemoveFinalMeasurements`; `RemoveBarriers`; `LightCone` ("Remove the gates that do not affect the outcome of a measurement on a circuit"); `ConsolidateBlocks` ("Replace each block of consecutive gates by a single Unitary node"); `Collect2qBlocks`; `RemoveResetInZeroState`; `ElidePermutations` ("Remove permutation operations from a pre-layout circuit"); `RemoveIdentityEquivalent` ("Remove gates with negligible effects"). — [Qiskit transpiler passes index](https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/docs/api/qiskit/transpiler_passes)
- `LightCone` accepts either the circuit's existing measurements or an observable (`bit_terms` + `indices`) and returns the reduced circuit that "keeps the same expectation value". — [Qiskit LightCone docs](https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/docs/api/qiskit/qiskit.transpiler.passes.LightCone)
- Diagonal batching: diagonal gates commute with each other and can all be applied in one memory pass (Fatima–Markov). Aer's `DiagonalFusion` fuses runs of diagonal gates, including CX–diag–CX ladders, into one diagonal op of ≥ 3 qubits, and its cost model charges any diagonal span 1.0 regardless of width. — [arXiv:2008.00216 §4.2–4.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.00216); [fusion.hpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/main/src/transpile/fusion.hpp)
- On diagonal-heavy circuits such as QAOA, a diagonal-specialized simulator beats general fused simulators (more than 9.7× vs baseline). — [arXiv:2604.12256 §5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.12256)
- Qulacs' heavy optimizer uses per-qubit commuting-Pauli tags to commute gates into mergeable positions. — [arXiv:2011.13524 §5.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13524)
- "Elidable" ops: Aer treats `barrier`, `id`, `u0` as ignorable and skips them during fusion. — [fusion.hpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/main/src/transpile/fusion.hpp)
- SWAP gates and qubit permutations can be removed from simulation entirely by relabeling (`ElidePermutations`). This is the same mechanism a simulator uses with a logical→physical map. — [Qiskit passes index](https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/docs/api/qiskit/transpiler_passes)

### Inferences
- In a simulator, SWAP gates should never touch memory. Update the logical→physical qubit map instead. The map is also what cache blocking uses.
- Ordering of passes: (1) remove barriers/identities; (2) relabel SWAPs; (3) light-cone prune (for an expectation of a k-local observable, or for measured-qubit subsets) and drop unused qubits, which shrinks n and so halves memory per qubit removed; (4) remove diagonals before final Z-basis measurement; (5) inverse/commutative cancellation; (6) 1-qubit chain merge; (7) diagonal batching; (8) blocking/staging; (9) fusion DP within stages.
- If a qubit is never entangled with the rest (a product-state factor), simulate it separately and tensor at readout. This is the extreme case of the Wang et al. "entanglement-free block" idea.

### Gaps
- I found no published quantitative study of how much each pre-pass reduces simulation time on standard benchmark suites (MQT Bench etc.).

## 5. Sampling many shots, mid-circuit measurement, and noise trajectories

### Takeaway
If all measurements are terminal and there is no noise, simulate once and draw all shots from |ψ|² (Aer's "measure sampling", qsim's sampling). Mid-circuit measurement or reset breaks this. One option is to re-simulate per shot from the first non-terminal measurement. Another is to branch shots by outcome ("shot branching") so that identical prefixes are shared. Noise is handled by quantum trajectories. qsim's delayed-inner-product trick defers Kraus application when the norm lower bound allows it, which keeps gate fusion intact and gives about 10× at low noise.

### Cited Findings
- Aer's statevector method samples from "ideal circuits with all measurements at end of the circuit". For noisy runs, "each shot samples a randomly sampled noisy circuit from the noise model". `statevector_sample_measure_opt` (default 10) is the qubit threshold above which a "large qubit optimized implementation" of measurement sampling is used. — [AerSimulator docs (older release)](https://qiskit.org/documentation/stubs/qiskit_aer.AerSimulator.html); [current docs](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-aer/stubs/qiskit_aer.AerSimulator.html)
- Aer `shot_branching_enable` (default False): "applying shot-branching technique to speed up multi-shots". `shot_branching_sampling_enable` applies sampling measure when the circuit's measurements allow it, which "improves speed to get counts for multiple-shots sharing the same state". `runtime_parameter_bind_enable` binds parameters at runtime "by using multi-shots without constructing circuits". `batched_shots_gpu` batches shots on GPU. — [AerSimulator docs](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-aer/stubs/qiskit_aer.AerSimulator.html)
- qsim trajectories: one Kraus operator is chosen per channel per trajectory, and errors scale as 1/√r over r trajectories ("typically in the thousands"). The delayed-inner-product algorithm uses lower bounds p̄_i (smallest singular value squared of K_i) with s = Σ p̄_i. If a uniform r < s, the operator is chosen without computing ⟨ψ|K†K|ψ⟩ and its application is deferred, "Deferring the operator application allows us to make use of gate fusion". Otherwise deferred ops are fused and applied and the conventional procedure runs. For mixed-unitary channels (e.g. depolarizing) s = 1, so the channel is always deferred. Result: "an order of magnitude runtime speedup for low noise with 27 qubits". "High noise prevents gate fusion and limits the arithmetic intensity." — [arXiv:2111.02396 §III.E–F](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)
- qsim's fusers treat measurements as fusion-window boundaries. Measurements at equal times are merged, and fusion never crosses them. — [fuser_mqubit.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/quantumlib/qsim/master/lib/fuser_mqubit.h); [fuser_basic.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/quantumlib/qsim/master/lib/fuser_basic.h)
- Aer's fusion segments stop at ops the method cannot fuse. When noise (Kraus/superop) is present and allowed, Aer can switch to Kraus or superop fusion methods (`fusion_allow_kraus`, `fusion_allow_superop`, both default false). — [fusion.hpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/main/src/transpile/fusion.hpp)

### Inferences
- Sampling S shots from a 2^n state: compute the probabilities in one streaming pass (parallel prefix sums per block plus block totals), draw S uniforms, sort them, and merge-walk the cumulative distribution. That is O(2^n + S log S) with one extra sweep. An alias table is O(2^n) to build with O(1) per shot. Either way it is about one sweep, versus S full simulations.
- Marginal sampling: if only m < n qubits are measured, permute (via the logical→physical map) so that the measured qubits are the high bits. Then the per-outcome probability is a sum over contiguous blocks, which streams efficiently.
- Mid-circuit measurement with many shots: simulate the prefix once, then branch at each measurement into 2 states weighted by outcome counts drawn binomially among the remaining shots (the shot-branching idea). The number of distinct states is bounded by min(shots, 2^#measurements). With a 62 GB budget at 30+ qubits, branches must be processed depth-first, one at a time.
- Noise trajectories: run trajectories in parallel across cores only when the state is small (≤ ~22–24 qubits, cache-resident). For large states, parallelize within each trajectory. Adopt qsim's deferral so that fusion is preserved between rare non-unitary Kraus events.

### Gaps
- I did not find a primary paper describing Aer's shot-branching algorithm or the details of `statevector_sample_measure_opt`. Only option descriptions were verified.
- I found no published comparison of sampling algorithms (sorted-uniform merge vs alias vs binary search) inside state-vector simulators.

## 6. Published quantitative comparisons of fusion and partitioning strategies

### Takeaway
Head-to-head comparisons are scarce and mostly GPU-based. Atlas compares its DP kernelizer against greedy 5-qubit fusion and HyQuas/cuQuantum. Häner–Steiger compare kmax = 3/4/5 cluster counts. Qulacs reports that 2-qubit merging is optimal on its CPUs for random circuits. qsim's docs give qualitative f-guidance. Wang et al. 2026 compare naive, fused, hybrid and their combined approach on clusters. Optimal fusion width is machine- and circuit-dependent, and every serious system benchmarks to set it.

### Cited Findings
- Atlas KERNELIZE vs greedy fusion up to 5 qubits (geomean over 9 sizes, 28–36 qubits, per family): greedy is competitive only on dj and qsvm. Atlas end-to-end: up to 20.2× (4.0× avg) over HyQuas, 7.2× (3.2× avg) over cuQuantum, and up to 105× with DRAM offload against offloading simulators. — [arXiv:2408.09055 §VII](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09055)
- Häner–Steiger, cluster counts for kmax = 3/4/5 (30-qubit depth-25 circuit, 369 gates): about 82 / 46 / 36 (parsed from the PDF text; treat the exact mapping of numbers to columns with care). — [arXiv:1704.01127 Table 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.01127)
- qsim: f = 4 is usually optimal for large circuits and many threads, 2–3 for small circuits or few threads, and 6 rarely. — [qsim docs](https://quantumai.google/qsim/choose_hw); [arXiv:2111.02396](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02396)
- Qulacs: merging to at most 2 qubits is "typically optimal" for random circuits on its test CPUs. Heavy vs light optimization benefit "varies depending on the structure of quantum circuits and computing devices". — [arXiv:2011.13524 §5.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13524)
- Wang et al. 2026: fusion alone gave 2.4× on QFT. The hybrid approach gave 8.5× over naive. The full approach (cache blocking + merge booster + fusion) gave 34× over naive and 5.8× over hybrid at 36 qubits, with up to 160× on circuit-level benchmarks per the abstract. — [arXiv:2604.12256](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.12256)
- Doi–Horii cache blocking vs non-blocked chunk exchange: +20% (QV) and +80% (QFT) at 33–35 qubits with chunks spilling to host memory. — [arXiv:2102.02957 §V](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.02957)
- HiSVSIM acyclic partitioning: up to 3.9× (2.1× avg) single-level and up to 5.7× multi-level over its baseline. — [arXiv:2205.06973](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.06973)

### Inferences
- The consistent lessons are: (1) cost-model-driven DP beats fixed-width greedy when the cost table is measured on the actual hardware; (2) diagonal gates need their own path (cost ≈ 1 sweep regardless of width); (3) the biggest wins at large n come from reducing the number of full-state DRAM sweeps through staging/blocking, not from fusion width alone; (4) k = 4–5 is the consensus sweet spot for dense fused kernels on wide-SIMD hardware, and k = 2–3 for cache-resident or few-thread cases.

### Gaps
- No primary source was found for "Gate fusion is map coloring for quantum circuit simulation", "Diffuse", "QuEST fused", or an IBM/Ishikawa Aer fusion paper. These may not exist under those names or were not indexed.
- No CPU-only, same-machine comparison of qsim vs Aer vs Qulacs fusion strategies at fixed n was found in primary literature.
